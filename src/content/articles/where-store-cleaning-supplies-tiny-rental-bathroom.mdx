A tiny rental bathroom has no broom closet, no utility shelf, and usually no spare cabinet — so cleaning supplies end up on the floor, on the toilet tank, or in a damp pile under the sink where they leak, get forgotten, and make cleaning take twice as long. Good small apartment cleaning supply storage is not about buying more products. It is about giving the three or four products you actually need one defined, upright, reachable home that stays out of the shower spray and out of reach of children. This guide is part of [renter-friendly bathroom storage ideas for tiny bathrooms](/bathroom-storage/renter-friendly-bathroom-storage-ideas-tiny-bathrooms/), and it builds on the cabinet method in [under-sink bathroom storage for small rental apartments](/bathroom-storage/under-sink-bathroom-storage-small-rental-apartments/).

Start by editing your supplies down to what you actually use in the bathroom: a bathroom or all-purpose spray, a toilet cleaner, a glass cleaner, a scrub brush, and two or three cloths. Anything you use less than once a month belongs in a closet or a hallway cupboard, not in the bathroom.

## 1. A Handled Caddy on a High Shelf

A handled caddy on a high over-toilet shelf is the best home for cleaning supplies in most tiny bathrooms: reachable for an adult, out of reach of small children, and easy to carry to the kitchen when you clean the whole apartment.

**Required space:** A shelf roughly 12 inches (30 cm) wide and 8 inches (20 cm) deep, at least 60 inches (152 cm) above the floor, with enough height above it for the tallest spray bottle — usually 12–14 inches (30–36 cm) of clearance. An over-toilet shelf unit works well for this.

**Installation:** None. Place a leak-proof tray on the shelf, set the caddy in the tray, and group products by task inside it. Label the caddy if you share the bathroom, so nobody borrows a bottle and leaves it in another room.

**Approximate dimensions:** A cleaning caddy is typically 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) long, 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) wide, and 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) tall — big enough for two or three spray bottles, a scrub brush, and a folded cloth.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Never store cleaning products above food, above the toilet tank lid in a way that risks a spill into the bowl, or above a child's reach if the shelf is low. Keep bleach and ammonia in separate spots and never mix them, and keep everything upright so caps do not leak.

**Pros:** Cleaning takes half the time when supplies are together and reachable; the caddy carries to the kitchen or living room; a high shelf keeps chemicals away from children and pets.

**Cons:** Takes a shelf that could hold towels; a caddy left on the floor becomes a trip hazard; some products need ventilation, so do not seal them in an airtight box.

**Suitable products:** A handled cleaning caddy and a small leak-proof tray, typically approximately $10–$20 combined.

## 2. A Defined Section of the Under-Sink Cabinet

If you prefer supplies out of sight, give them a defined section of the under-sink cabinet — not the whole cabinet, and not a loose pile. One side of the cabinet, in a tray, with a tension rod above it for spray bottles, is enough.

**Required space:** A cabinet section roughly 12 inches (30 cm) wide and 10 inches (25 cm) deep, on one side of the drain trap, with at least 12 inches (30 cm) of height for spray bottles. Measure around the trap before you commit to this spot.

**Installation:** Line the section with a leak mat, set a tray in it, and place the caddy or the bottles upright in the tray. Wedge a short tension rod across the cabinet above the tray to hang one or two spray bottles by their triggers if space is tight.

**Approximate dimensions:** A tray for this use is typically 12–14 inches (30–36 cm) long, 8–10 inches (20–25 cm) wide, and 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) tall, with a raised edge to contain drips.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Keep products upright, keep a leak mat under the pipes, and check monthly for drips — a slow leak can soak cleaning cloths and grow mold unnoticed. Do not store anything against a hot water pipe, and never store cleaning products in the same bin as medicines or personal care items.

**Pros:** Supplies are out of sight and out of the way; the tray catches leaks before they reach the cabinet floor; the cabinet door keeps chemicals away from children if it has a latch.

**Cons:** You have to crouch to reach supplies; a damp cabinet is not ideal for products that need ventilation; a leak can go unnoticed if you do not check monthly.

**Suitable products:** A leak mat, a shallow tray, and a small caddy or a set of stackable bins, typically approximately $20–$35 for the set. ## 3. A Slim Rolling Cart Beside the Toilet or Vanity

A narrow rolling cart turns the gap beside the toilet or vanity into a cleaning station: sprays on one tier, cloths and a brush on another, and a small trash bag on the bottom.

**Required space:** A gap at least 6 inches (15 cm) wide and 15 inches (38 cm) deep. Measure the gap at floor level and at counter height, because baseboards can narrow it by an inch.

**Installation:** Assemble the cart, which usually snaps together without tools, and roll it into place. Put the heaviest bottles on the bottom tier so it does not tip when you pull it out, and keep the cart clear of the toilet flush handle and the shut-off valve.

**Approximate dimensions:** Typically 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) wide, 15–18 inches (38–46 cm) deep, and 30–35 inches (76–89 cm) tall, with three or four tiers.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Freestanding and fully removable. Keep cleaning products upright in the cart, do not overload the top tier, and do not block the toilet tank lid or the shut-off valve.

**Pros:** Supplies are together, visible, and easy to pull out; the cart rolls out for cleaning behind it; a cart tier can also hold toilet paper and extra toiletries, so one piece serves two jobs.

**Cons:** Narrow tiers fit only small bottles; a cart left in the open looks cluttered if overfilled; wheels can mark vinyl flooring if grit gets trapped under them.

**Suitable products:** A slim three- or four-tier rolling cart, typically approximately $20–$35.

## 4. An Over-Door Rack for Tools, Not Chemicals

An over-door rack on the bathroom door is a good home for cleaning tools — a scrub brush, a squeegee, gloves, and cloths — but not for chemical sprays, which should stay upright on a shelf or in a cabinet.

**Required space:** A standard interior door, usually 1 3/8 inches (3.5 cm) thick, with at least 1/4 inch (0.6 cm) of clearance above the door so the hooks do not scrape the frame. Check that the door still closes and locks with the rack in place.

**Installation:** Hang the rack over the top of the door. No screws or adhesive are needed, and removal takes under a minute.

**Approximate dimensions:** Racks are typically 16–18 inches (41–46 cm) wide with three to five hooks or two wire baskets, hanging 24–36 inches (61–91 cm) down the door.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Do not overload the rack — most are rated for approximately 10–20 pounds (4.5–9 kg) in total. Hang tools only, never chemical sprays, which can leak if tipped, and never hang wet cloths on a hollow-core door for weeks at a time.

**Pros:** Zero installation and zero damage; keeps tools visible and dry; frees a shelf or a cabinet tier for bottles.

**Cons:** Can make the door feel heavy; wire baskets can snag cloths; not suitable for chemicals or for anything heavy.

**Suitable products:** An over-door multi-hook rack, typically approximately $15–$25.

## 5. A Small Laundry and Cleaning Nook Outside the Bathroom

In many small apartments, the honest answer is that the bathroom is too small for all of your cleaning supplies — and the best storage is just outside it. A shelf, a cart, or a closet nook in the hallway or laundry area can hold the full kit, with only a small caddy kept in the bathroom for daily use.

**Required space:** A hallway shelf, a closet shelf, or a cart nook roughly 18 inches (46 cm) wide and 12 inches (30 cm) deep, within a few steps of the bathroom door. The spot should be dry, ventilated, and out of reach of small children.

**Installation:** None beyond placing a caddy or a set of bins on the shelf. Keep the daily-use caddy in the bathroom and the backups — refills, extra cloths, and products you use weekly rather than daily — in the nook.

**Approximate dimensions:** A hallway shelf for this use is typically 18–24 inches (46–61 cm) wide and 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) deep; a caddy for backups is about 12 inches (30 cm) long and 8 inches (20 cm) wide.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Freestanding shelves and carts only — no drilling. If you use a tension-rod or adhesive shelf in the hallway, follow the same weight and surface rules as in the bathroom. For laundry-area ideas, see [small apartment laundry storage solutions for renters](/renter-friendly-solutions/small-apartment-laundry-storage-solutions-renters/).

**Pros:** Frees the bathroom for toiletries and towels; keeps the full kit together in a dry, ventilated spot; a single caddy carries from the nook to any room you are cleaning.

**Cons:** Supplies are not in the bathroom when you need them mid-clean; a hallway shelf can look cluttered if it is not contained in bins; you need the discipline to return the caddy after each use.

**Suitable products:** A small freestanding shelf or rolling cart and two labeled bins, typically approximately $25–$50 for the set.

## 6. A Labeled Bin System for Shared Bathrooms

If you share the bathroom with roommates, unlabeled supplies get borrowed, mixed up, and left in other rooms. A simple labeled bin system — one bin per person or one bin per task — prevents that.

**Required space:** A shelf or cabinet section roughly 18 inches (46 cm) wide and 10 inches (25 cm) deep, divided into two or three bins of about 6 inches (15 cm) each.

**Installation:** None. Place the bins on the shelf, label each one with a label maker or masking tape, and agree with roommates that supplies stay in their bin and return to the shelf after use.

**Approximate dimensions:** Small labeled bins are typically 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) long, 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) wide, and 5–7 inches (13–18 cm) tall.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Fully removable. Keep bleach and ammonia in separate bins and never mix them, keep everything upright, and keep the bins out of reach of small children.

**Pros:** Ends the borrowed-bottle problem; makes it obvious when a product runs out; a shared system is easier to keep tidy than a shared pile.

**Cons:** Needs roommate agreement to work; bins take up shelf space; a system that is too detailed — a bin for every product — becomes harder to maintain than a simple one.

**Suitable products:** Three small stackable bins and a label maker or masking tape, typically approximately $15–$25.

## 7. A Squeegee, Brush, and Cloth Station by the Shower

Daily tools — a squeegee, a scrub brush, and a cloth — deserve their own spot by the shower, so a quick wipe-down after a shower takes 30 seconds instead of a trip to fetch supplies.

**Required space:** A small adhesive hook or a suction hook on smooth tile beside the shower, or a narrow shelf about 8 inches (20 cm) wide, within arm's reach of the shower door.

**Installation:** Clean the tile with rubbing alcohol, press the adhesive hook firmly for 30 seconds, wait the full curing hour, then hang the squeegee and brush. A suction hook works only on smooth glossy tile — test it first.

**Approximate dimensions:** A squeegee is typically 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) wide; a scrub brush is about 8–10 inches (20–25 cm) long. The station needs about 12 inches (30 cm) of wall width in total.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Use adhesive or suction only on smooth glazed tile, stay well under the weight limit, and check the hold weekly. Never hang anything heavy from a suction hook, and remove adhesive by pulling the tab slowly along the wall.

**Pros:** A 30-second wipe after each shower prevents mold and soap scum, so deep cleaning takes half the time; tools dry faster when hung rather than left on the tub edge.

**Cons:** Very small capacity; adhesive and suction both weaken in steam, so check the hold weekly; a station that is too close to the spray gets soaked every shower.

**Suitable products:** A squeegee, a scrub brush, and one or two removable adhesive hooks, typically approximately $15–$25 combined.

## Common Mistakes

**Storing cleaning products on the shower floor or the toilet tank.** Products left on the shower floor get knocked over, diluted, and forgotten, and anything on the tank can fall into the bowl. Give every product a shelf, a tray, or a cart tier — never the floor or the tank.

**Mixing bleach and ammonia.** This is the most dangerous mistake in this guide. Never mix bleach with ammonia or with any other cleaner, and never store them in the same caddy section where a leak could mix them. Keep them in separate spots, label them clearly, and use one product at a time with the bathroom ventilated.

**Keeping every product you have ever bought.** A small bathroom does not need eight sprays. Edit down to three or four products you actually use, move the rest to a closet or give them away, and you will free a whole shelf in one move.

**Storing supplies in a damp, sealed box.** Cleaning products need ventilation, and a sealed box in a humid bathroom traps moisture and grows mold. Use an open caddy or a ventilated bin, and never seal cleaning cloths away while they are still damp.

**Forgetting the leak check.** A slow drip under the sink can soak cleaning cloths and cardboard packaging for weeks before you notice. Check the cabinet monthly, feel the floor for moisture, and report a leak to your landlord promptly.

## Conclusion

The single best first step is to buy a handled caddy, put your three or four daily cleaning products in it upright, and place it on a high shelf — ideally an over-toilet shelf — with a leak-proof tray underneath. That one move gives every product a home, keeps chemicals out of reach of children, and lets you carry the whole kit to any room in seconds.
