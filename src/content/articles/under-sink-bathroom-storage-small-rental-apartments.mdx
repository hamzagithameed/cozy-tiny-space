The space under a bathroom sink is not a smaller version of the space under a kitchen sink, and treating it like one is why most organizers disappoint. Getting under sink bathroom storage small apartment renters can actually live with starts with two facts. First, you may not have a cabinet at all — plenty of small rental bathrooms have a pedestal sink with bare pipes and open floor. Second, even when a vanity cabinet exists, it is shallower than a kitchen cabinet, choked by a trap in the middle, and filled with personal-care items in a room that steams up every single day. This guide solves both cases separately, because they are different problems. It sits inside the wider plan in our [renter-friendly bathroom storage ideas for tiny bathrooms](/bathroom-storage/renter-friendly-bathroom-storage-ideas-tiny-bathrooms/), which covers the over-toilet space, the shower, and the walls as well. ApartmentGuide's [under-sink storage solutions](https://www.apartmentguide.com/blog/under-sink-storage-solutions/) cover the same pipe-first logic for both sinks.

One rule runs through everything below: nothing under a bathroom sink gets fixed, glued, or wedged in place. Every organizer here lifts out in under a minute, because the shutoff valves and trap live in the same space and a slow leak is the most common disaster in a small rental bathroom. Storage that blocks the pipes is not storage — it is a future deposit deduction.

## First, Work Out Which Sink You Actually Have

Stand in front of your sink and look down. You have one of two setups, and they need opposite approaches.

**A pedestal sink** is a basin on a narrow column, typically 20–24 inches (51–61 cm) wide at the top with a column only 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) across. There is no cabinet, no door, and no hidden space — just open floor on either side of the column, exposed supply lines, and a trap you can see. Storage here means adding small furniture around the pedestal, and everything you store stays visible.

**A vanity cabinet** is an enclosed box, usually 24–30 inches (61–76 cm) wide in a small apartment, with an interior depth of about 18–21 inches (46–53 cm). That sounds generous until you open the door: the drain trap hangs in the center, two supply lines and their shutoff valves take up the back, and the usable space is really two narrow columns on either side of the plumbing plus a shallow strip in front of it.

A wall-hung sink — a basin floating on brackets with no column — behaves like a pedestal for storage purposes: open underneath, nothing enclosed.

The contrast with the kitchen matters. A kitchen under-sink cabinet is deeper (usually 24 inches / 61 cm), holds cleaning chemicals, a trash can, and big bottles, and stays dry most of the day. The bathroom version holds smaller, more personal items, sits in the most humid room in the home, and in the pedestal case has no enclosure at all. If you want the kitchen equivalent of this guide, our [under-sink storage guide for small apartment kitchens](/kitchen-storage/under-sink-storage-small-apartments-renters-guide/) uses the same measure-first approach on a bigger, drier cabinet. Here, the measurements shrink and the moisture rules get stricter.

## Measure the Space the Pipes Leave You

Every failed under-sink organizer was bought before anyone measured. Do this once, write the numbers on your phone, and take a photo of the pipes — the photo alone will save you a return trip.

![Hands measuring the space under a bathroom sink around the pipes with a tape measure](/images/bathroom-storage/under-sink-bathroom-storage-small-rental-apartments-5.webp)

For a **vanity cabinet**, take four numbers:

- **Total interior width** — door frame to door frame, not the outside of the cabinet.
- **Clear width on each side of the trap** — the trap is rarely centered, so measure left and right separately. On a 24-inch (61 cm) vanity, expect roughly 7–10 inches (18–25 cm) per side.
- **Height from the cabinet floor to the underside of the trap** — this is your stacking ceiling. On many small vanities it is only 10–13 inches (25–33 cm), which means two 5-inch (13 cm) bins stack, and nothing taller fits under the trap at all.
- **Depth from the inside of the door to the back wall** — bathroom vanities are shallow, and a bin that fit your kitchen cabinet will often be 3–4 inches (8–10 cm) too deep here.

For a **pedestal sink**, measure the open floor on each side of the column (usually 6–10 inches / 15–25 cm before you hit a wall or the toilet), the width of the column itself, and the height from the floor to the underside of the basin. That last number decides whether a two-tier shelf fits or only a single low one.

While you are down there, note where the shutoff valves sit and how far the trap swings toward the door. Any organizer that ends up pressed against a valve or the trap has to go back, no matter how neatly it fits everywhere else.

## Pedestal Sinks: Building Storage Where There Is No Cabinet

With no cabinet, your job is to add the smallest possible furniture in the exact shape of the gap. Three pieces do almost all of the work.

### The U-shaped shelf

The single most useful pedestal product is a U-shaped shelf: a rectangular frame with a notch cut out of the back or middle so it straddles the column and the trap. Typical two-tier versions are 20–24 inches (51–61 cm) wide overall, stand 20–24 inches (51–61 cm) tall, and cost roughly $25–$45. The top tier sits just under the basin and holds daily items at a height you can actually reach; the bottom tier takes spare bottles and a small first-aid box.

![U-shaped under-sink shelf fitting around pedestal pipes for under sink bathroom storage in a small apartment](/images/bathroom-storage/under-sink-bathroom-storage-small-rental-apartments-2.webp)

Check the notch width against your column measurement before buying — columns vary, and a shelf whose cutout is 2 inches (5 cm) too narrow will not sit flat. Adjustable-width versions, where the two halves slide, forgive more measurement error and are worth the small price difference.

### The wrap-around caddy

A pedestal caddy is a shallow tray or set of hooks that wraps around the basin or hangs from its rim, holding the toothbrush, soap, and the two or three products you use every morning. Expect to pay $12–$25. It holds very little — that is the point. On a pedestal sink, counter space barely exists, and a caddy keeps the daily kit off the rim of the basin where it otherwise slides into the bowl.

![Pedestal sink wrap-around caddy holding toiletries in a small rental bathroom with no cabinet](/images/bathroom-storage/under-sink-bathroom-storage-small-rental-apartments-4.webp)

### Skirts, baskets, and the gap beside the pedestal

A fabric pedestal skirt with pockets ($12–$20) hides the pipes and adds pocket storage, but be honest about the trade-offs: it collects dust, it can look untidy fast, and it has to come off completely for any plumbing work. A lidded basket on the floor beside the pedestal is usually the better buy — it hides backstock, shrugs off splashes if you choose plastic or resin over untreated wicker, and carries straight out of the room on cleaning day.

If there is a gap of 5 inches (13 cm) or more between the pedestal and the wall or toilet, a narrow rolling cart made for gaps ($20–$35) turns dead floor into three or four shelves. Measure the gap at floor level, not at basin height — walls and skirting boards narrow it.

The honest limit of pedestal storage: it is all visible and it all sits in the splash zone. Choose containers with lids, keep only daily and weekly items here, and store true backstock somewhere drier, like a hallway closet. Leave a hand's width of bare floor around the column itself, too. That gap looks like wasted space, but it is where you mop, where you spot a drip forming at the trap before it spreads, and where a plumber's wrench needs to swing. A pedestal setup that fills every visible inch has quietly failed at the one job that matters most in a rental bathroom: keeping the plumbing easy to reach, easy to clean around, and easy to inspect.

## Vanity Cabinets: Layering Around the Trap

A vanity cabinet organizes in layers, working from the floor up and treating the trap as a fixed obstacle, not an enemy.

### Layer one: the floor

Start with a waterproof under-sink mat cut to the cabinet floor ($8–$15). It protects the cabinet from the drips and spills that are guaranteed in this spot, and a mat with a raised lip turns a slow leak into a visible puddle you will notice instead of a soaked, swollen cabinet floor you will not. On top of the mat, place one low bin on each side of the trap: 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) long, 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) wide, 5–7 inches (13–18 cm) tall. Left bin for hair and body products, right bin for skincare and first aid, or whatever split matches your routine.

![Stackable bins inside a small bathroom vanity cabinet around the trap](/images/bathroom-storage/under-sink-bathroom-storage-small-rental-apartments-3.webp)

### Layer two: stack or bridge

If your floor-to-trap height is 11 inches (28 cm) or more, you can add a second level. Two honest ways to do it:

- **Stack a second bin** on each side — but only if you can lift the top bin out without tipping it, and the stack stays clear of the trap and valves.
- **Bridge over the trap** with an adjustable under-sink shelf frame: two small shelves on legs with a gap in the middle where the pipes pass. These typically adjust from 16–24 inches (41–61 cm) wide and cost $18–$30. They use the dead air above the bins in front of the trap, which is the one spot simple stacking wastes.

### Layer three: slide

The deepest, darkest corner of a vanity is the back of the wider side. A narrow sliding two-tier organizer — typically 8–10 inches (20–25 cm) wide, on a frame that pulls toward you — costs $20–$35 and is the one organizer that genuinely changes daily life, because everything on it arrives at the front of the cabinet instead of you kneeling to dig for it. Put your most-used products on the slider and the rarely touched spares in the static bins.

![Small sliding organizer pulled out from under a rental bathroom sink](/images/bathroom-storage/under-sink-bathroom-storage-small-rental-apartments-7.webp)

A short tension rod ($6–$10) across the cabinet can hold the one or two spray bottles a bathroom actually needs, hung by their triggers — but most bathrooms need far less hanging storage than kitchens do, so treat the rod as optional, not essential.

Do not ignore the shallow strip of floor in front of the trap, between the plumbing and the door. It is usually only 3–5 inches (8–13 cm) deep, too tight for a bin, but a flat tray there catches the small things that otherwise migrate around the cabinet — a spare razor, cotton pads, a nail clipper kit. A tray also keeps those items off the mat, so the mat stays visible for leak checks. It is a small zone, but in a cabinet this size, small zones are all you get, and assigning each one a job is the whole method.

Trade-off to accept: every layer you add hides the layer beneath it and slows down leak spotting. Two layers is the practical maximum under a bathroom trap; a third layer is how cabinets become junk drawers again.

## The Inside of the Door: The Only Dry Vertical Surface

The inside of the vanity door is vertical, dry, and almost always empty. An over-door rack or a slim door-mounted basket ($10–$18) holds a hair dryer, a flat iron, or tall bottles that do not fit under the trap.

![Over-door rack inside a bathroom vanity door with hair tools and bottles](/images/bathroom-storage/under-sink-bathroom-storage-small-rental-apartments-8.webp)

Two checks before you buy. First, clearance: a door rack adds 3–4 inches (8–10 cm) of depth inside the cabinet, and on shallow vanities the rack will hit the trap every time the door closes. Measure the gap between the closed door and the trap, and compare it with the rack's depth. Second, the door itself: most vanity doors are about 3/4 inch (19 mm) thick and take an over-door hook easily, but skip adhesive-mounted racks on painted cabinet doors unless you have tested that the adhesive releases cleanly — a torn paint patch inside a cabinet door is still damage. Over-door hooks that simply hang are the rental-safe choice.

One safety note: hair tools go on the door rack only after they have cooled, and let cords hang inside the cabinet rather than trailing onto the floor, where they sit in splash water.

## What Belongs Down There — and What the Humidity Ruins

Under-sink bathroom storage works when the contents match the conditions: dark, enclosed (or fully open, for pedestals), and damp after every shower.

**Belongs there:** daily toiletries and hair care, first aid, spare toilet paper in a lidded bin raised off the floor, the bathroom's own cleaning spray, and backstock of shampoo and soap in sealed bottles.

**Does not belong there:** medicines and supplements that need a cool, dry place — the bathroom cabinet is the most humid spot in the home and the worst medicine cabinet despite the name. Makeup you want to keep hygienic. Anything in cardboard packaging, which goes soft and then moldy. Chargers, electric toothbrush bases, and trimmers stored loose on the cabinet floor. And nothing, ever, resting against a hot water supply line.

Materials follow the same logic. Plastic, resin, and powder-coated metal survive bathroom humidity; plain steel rusts at the joints, and untreated wood or bamboo swells and spots in a steamy room unless it is sealed and kept out of the direct splash zone. Lids matter more here than in any other cabinet — they keep the steam's condensation off the contents, not just the dust.

Cleaning supplies deserve one sentence of their own: keep the bathroom's bottle or two under the sink if you must, but a full cleaning kit stored in the dampest cabinet in the home helps nobody. ## Keep the Pipes Reachable: Leak Access Is Non-Negotiable

Everything above was chosen to be removable, and this section is why. The trap and both shutoff valves live in the same few inches as your storage. When — not if — something under there drips, you or a plumber need the space empty and the valves reachable in about a minute.

Build these habits in from the start:

- **Keep the valves visible.** Never stack a bin in front of a shutoff valve. If you cannot see both valves when you open the door, rearrange until you can.
- **Let the mat do its job.** The waterproof mat from layer one is leak detection, not just protection: water pools on it visibly instead of soaking silently into the cabinet floor.
- **Check monthly.** Run the tap for thirty seconds, look at the trap joints, and run a finger along the underside of the trap and around each valve. Dry means fine; damp means a slow leak worth reporting to your landlord now, while it is a washer and not a rotten cabinet.
- **Know your removal order.** Bins out first, then the bridge shelf or slider, then nothing — because nothing is attached. If emptying your cabinet takes longer than a minute or requires tools, the setup is wrong for a rental.

There is a deposit angle here too: a leak you spot and report early is the landlord's repair. A leak that soaked through a cabinet floor for months behind a wall of fixed storage looks, on move-out day, a lot like neglect.

## Two Worked Setups, One per Sink Type

**Pedestal setup (about $40–$55):** a two-tier U-shaped shelf ($30), a lidded basket beside the pedestal for backstock ($10), and a wrap-around caddy for the daily kit ($15). Daily items live on the caddy and the shelf's top tier; everything else is in the basket, off the floor and out of the splash zone.

**Vanity setup (about $50–$70):** a waterproof mat ($10), a set of four stackable bins ($20), a sliding two-tier organizer on the wider side ($25), and an over-door rack for the hair dryer ($12). Bins flank the trap, the slider handles daily products, the door takes the tall and hot items, and both valves stay visible.

Both setups stay inside the budget at the top of this page, both empty out in under a minute, and both leave the pipes exactly as exposed as the day you moved in.

## Where Good Storage Goes Wrong

- **Buying before measuring the trap.** Width alone tells you almost nothing; the trap position and the floor-to-trap height decide what fits. Measure all four numbers first.
- **Shopping in the kitchen aisle.** Kitchen under-sink bins are built for a 24-inch-deep (61 cm) cabinet. In an 18-inch-deep (46 cm) vanity they jam against the door or the trap. Bathroom-depth organizers exist — buy those.
- **Storing medicine in the "medicine cabinet" spot.** Humidity degrades medication and supplements. The under-sink cabinet is the worst place in the home for them, whatever tradition says.
- **Burying the shutoff valves.** A bin wedged in front of a valve turns a small drip into a flooded bathroom while you dig. Valves stay visible and reachable, always.
- **Choosing pretty materials over waterproof ones.** Untreated wood, plain steel, and cardboard organizers all fail in bathroom humidity — rust, swelling, and mold, usually within one season of showers.
- **Filling the pedestal floor completely.** Wall-to-wall baskets around a pedestal mean you cannot clean the floor, cannot see a leak, and cannot reach the trap. Leave air around the column.
- **Gluing organizers inside a rental cabinet.** Adhesive pads on painted cabinet interiors can tear the finish on removal. Freestanding and over-door only — if it needs glue or screws, it does not belong in a rental under-sink.
