The cabinet under a bathroom sink looks like storage, but in a small rental apartment it usually works like a junk drawer: pipes in the middle, bottles tipped over, and everything you need at the back. Good under sink bathroom storage small apartment renters can actually use is not about buying more containers — it is about measuring around the pipes, giving every category a defined spot, and choosing organizers that pull out instead of forcing you to crouch and dig. This guide is part of the broader plan in [renter-friendly bathroom storage ideas for tiny bathrooms](/bathroom-storage/renter-friendly-bathroom-storage-ideas-tiny-bathrooms/), and it pairs well with the over-toilet ideas in [how to add storage above a rental bathroom toilet without drilling](/bathroom-storage/storage-above-rental-bathroom-toilet-without-drilling/).

Start by emptying the cabinet completely, wiping it out, and checking for leaks. Then measure three things: the width of the cabinet on each side of the drain trap, the height from the cabinet floor to the underside of the trap, and the depth from the door to the back wall. Those numbers decide which organizers will fit — and they will save you at least one return trip.

## 1. Stackable Bins on Either Side of the Pipes

Low, clear, stackable bins are the workhorse of under-sink storage. They turn the floor of the cabinet into two pull-out drawers, one on each side of the pipes.

**Required space:** A cabinet floor at least 18 inches (46 cm) wide in total, with at least 8 inches (20 cm) of clear width on one side of the drain trap. The bins need 5–7 inches (13–18 cm) of height under the trap and any supply lines.

**Installation:** None. Line the cabinet floor with non-slip shelf liner, set the bins in place, and sort items by category — hair products in one bin, skin care in another, first aid in a third. Stack a second bin only if you can still lift it out without tipping the one below.

**Approximate dimensions:** Small stackable bins are typically 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) long, 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) wide, and 5–7 inches (13–18 cm) tall. Two bins side by side usually fill a standard 24-inch (61 cm) vanity.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Fully removable with no fixing. Do not stack bins so high that they press against the drain trap or supply lines, and do not store anything against a hot water pipe. Keep a small tray under any bottle that has leaked before.

**Pros:** Cheap, flexible, and easy to pull out like a drawer; clear bins let you see what you have; you can take a bin to the counter and put it back in seconds.

**Cons:** Bins slide if the cabinet floor is smooth and you skip the liner; stacking more than two high makes the bottom bin hard to reach; very small vanities may fit only one bin per side.

**Suitable products:** Clear stackable bins with handles, typically approximately $15–$30 for a set of four, plus a roll of non-slip shelf liner for approximately $5–$10.

## 2. Tension Rod for Spray Bottles

A spring tension rod wedged horizontally across the cabinet turns dead air space into hanging storage for spray bottles, which are otherwise the hardest items to store under a sink.

**Required space:** Two opposing cabinet walls 12–20 inches (30–51 cm) apart, with at least 8 inches (20 cm) of clear space below the rod for the bottle bodies to hang. The rod must sit above the drain trap, not across it.

**Installation:** Extend the rod until it presses firmly between the walls, then hang spray bottles by their trigger necks from the rod. Test the hold with one bottle first, and keep the total hanging weight under about 3 pounds (1.4 kg).

**Approximate dimensions:** Tension rods for this use are usually 12–20 inches (30–51 cm) long and about 3/4 inch (2 cm) in diameter. Most spray bottles need 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) of hanging clearance.

**Rental-friendly limits:** No drilling or adhesive, and the rod removes in seconds. Do not hang anything heavy, and do not wedge the rod so tightly that it bows the cabinet walls. Keep cleaning products upright and separate from personal care items.

**Pros:** Uses vertical space that bins cannot reach; spray bottles stay upright and easy to grab; costs under $10 and installs in a minute.

**Cons:** Only works for bottles with a trigger neck; a rod that is too long or too short for your cabinet will not hold; hanging bottles can swing and knock over bins when you open the door.

**Suitable products:** A spring tension rod, typically approximately $5–$10, in a length that matches your cabinet width. The same idea works for other rooms — see [tension rod storage ideas for renters](/renter-guides/tension-rod-storage-ideas-renters/).

## 3. Two-Tier Freestanding Shelf Around the Pipes

A two-tier shelf designed to fit around a drain trap — usually a U-shaped or split shelf — adds a second level inside the cabinet without any fixing.

**Required space:** A cabinet at least 20 inches (51 cm) wide and 14 inches (36 cm) deep, with a drain trap that sits high enough to leave 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) of height for the lower tier. Measure the trap height before buying, because traps vary by several inches.

**Installation:** Assemble the shelf on the floor, slide it into the cabinet around the pipes, and level it. No tools beyond the included hardware are normally needed, and no screws go into the cabinet.

**Approximate dimensions:** Typically 14–18 inches (36–46 cm) wide, 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) deep, and 12–16 inches (30–41 cm) tall, with two tiers spaced 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) apart.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Freestanding only — do not screw it to the cabinet walls. Leave a gap between the shelf and any supply line so vibration does not wear the pipe, and do not store anything against a hot water pipe.

**Pros:** Doubles usable space inside the cabinet; the top tier keeps daily items at a reachable height; the shelf lifts out for cleaning or a leak check.

**Cons:** The U-shaped cutout wastes some space; cheaper shelves wobble when loaded; a shelf that is too tall will not fit under a low trap.

**Suitable products:** Freestanding two-tier under-sink shelves with an adjustable cutout, typically approximately $20–$40.

## 4. Narrow Pull-Out Organizer in the Remaining Gap

Once bins and a shelf are in place, there is usually a narrow gap left — often 4 to 6 inches (10–15 cm) wide — beside the pipes. A slim pull-out organizer fills it with a drawer you can slide out.

**Required space:** A gap at least 4 inches (10 cm) wide, 14 inches (36 cm) deep, and 10 inches (25 cm) tall. Measure the gap at the cabinet floor and at the height of the drawer, because pipes can narrow it partway up.

**Installation:** Set the organizer in the gap and test the slide several times. No fixing is needed for most lightweight units, though a small non-slip pad underneath keeps it from shifting when you pull it out.

**Approximate dimensions:** Typically 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) wide, 14–16 inches (36–41 cm) deep, and 10–14 inches (25–36 cm) tall, with two or three tiers.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Fully removable. Keep the load light so the drawer slides smoothly, and do not force it past a pipe — if it catches, the gap is too narrow for that unit.

**Pros:** Turns a wasted sliver into a usable drawer; good for tall bottles, cleaning cloths, or a hair dryer; slides out so you can reach the back without crouching.

**Cons:** Very narrow capacity; drawers can jam if overloaded; some units are too tall for a low cabinet.

**Suitable products:** Slim pull-out organizers with two or three tiers, typically approximately $15–$30.

## 5. Leak Mat and Drip Tray Under the Pipes

A leak mat is not storage, but it protects everything you store. A thin waterproof mat under the trap catches slow drips before they warp the cabinet floor or grow mold.

**Required space:** A mat that covers the cabinet floor under the trap and supply lines, usually 16–20 inches (41–51 cm) wide and 12–16 inches (30–41 cm) deep. Trim the mat to fit around the pipes if needed.

**Installation:** Lay the mat flat on the cabinet floor before you place bins or shelves. No adhesive is needed — the weight of the organizers holds it in place.

**Approximate dimensions:** Mats are typically 16–24 inches (41–61 cm) wide, 12–18 inches (30–46 cm) deep, and under 1/4 inch (0.6 cm) thick, with a raised edge of about 1/2 inch (1.3 cm) to contain drips.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Fully removable and reusable. Check the mat monthly for standing water, and replace it if it becomes stained or warped. Never use a mat to hide an active leak — report a leak to your landlord promptly.

**Pros:** Catches drips early and protects the cabinet floor; makes it obvious when a leak starts; costs very little for the damage it can prevent.

**Cons:** Takes up a small amount of height inside the cabinet; a mat that is too large can bunch up and make bins sit unevenly.

**Suitable products:** A waterproof under-sink leak mat or shallow drip tray, typically approximately $10–$20.

## 6. Over-Door Organizer on the Cabinet Door

The inside of the cabinet door is free space. A small over-door rack or a removable adhesive caddy on it holds brushes, small tubes, and cleaning cloths where you can see them.

**Required space:** A cabinet door at least 12 inches (30 cm) wide with enough clearance inside the cabinet for a rack about 3 inches (8 cm) deep. Check that the door still closes with the rack loaded.

**Installation:** Hang the rack over the top of the door, or stick a removable adhesive caddy to a smooth door interior after cleaning it. No drilling is needed for either method.

**Approximate dimensions:** Door racks are typically 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) wide and 3–4 inches (8–10 cm) deep, with two or three pockets or hooks.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Keep the load light so the door hinges are not strained. Test adhesive removal on a hidden spot first if the door interior is painted, and never hang anything heavy from the door.

**Pros:** Uses space that is otherwise completely wasted; keeps small items visible; removes in seconds at move-out.

**Cons:** Very small capacity; a loaded rack can prevent the door from closing; adhesive can pull paint off a painted door interior.

**Suitable products:** Small over-door racks or removable adhesive door caddies, typically approximately $10–$20.

## 7. A Labeled Cleaning Caddy That Lives Under the Sink

Cleaning supplies deserve their own caddy, not a loose pile. A handled caddy with a leak-proof tray keeps sprays, cloths, and a scrub brush together and carries to the rest of the apartment when you clean.

**Required space:** A cabinet section roughly 12 inches (30 cm) wide and 8 inches (20 cm) deep, or a tier of a rolling cart beside the vanity. The caddy must sit upright with room for the tallest bottle.

**Installation:** None. Place a small tray under the caddy, group products by task, and label the caddy if you share the bathroom. For the full method, see [where to store cleaning supplies in a tiny rental bathroom](/bathroom-storage/where-store-cleaning-supplies-tiny-rental-bathroom/).

**Approximate dimensions:** A cleaning caddy is typically 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) long, 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) wide, and 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) tall — big enough for two or three spray bottles and a scrub brush.

**Rental-friendly limits:** Keep products upright, keep bleach and ammonia in separate spots and never mix them, and keep everything out of reach of small children. Do not store cleaning products against a hot water pipe.

**Pros:** Cleaning takes half the time when supplies are together; the caddy carries to the kitchen or living room; a tray catches leaks before they reach the cabinet floor.

**Cons:** Takes a section of cabinet that could hold toiletries; a caddy left on the floor becomes a trip hazard; some products need ventilation, so do not seal them in an airtight box.

**Suitable products:** A handled cleaning caddy and a small leak-proof tray, typically approximately $10–$20 combined.

## Common Mistakes

**Skipping the measurement step.** Pipes, traps, and supply lines vary so much between apartments that a shelf that fits one vanity will not fit another. Measure the width on each side of the trap and the height under it before you buy, and keep the receipt until you have test-fitted the organizer.

**Storing everything in one deep pile.** A cabinet with no bins is a cabinet where the item you need is always at the back. Give every category a bin, keep daily items at the front, and keep backups at the back or on a higher tier.

**Ignoring a slow leak.** A damp cabinet floor, a musty smell, or a stained mat means water is getting in somewhere. Check the trap and supply lines monthly, and report a leak to your landlord promptly — a small drip can warp a cabinet and grow mold in weeks.

**Overloading the cabinet door.** A door rack with heavy bottles strains the hinges and prevents the door from closing. Keep door storage light — brushes, cloths, and small tubes only — and move anything heavy to a bin on the cabinet floor.

**Storing medicines in a damp cabinet.** Bathrooms are humid, and the cabinet under the sink is the most humid spot of all. Store medicines in a cool, dry place outside the bathroom, and keep only daily toiletries under the sink.

## Conclusion

The single best first step under a rental bathroom sink is to empty the cabinet, lay down a leak mat and non-slip liner, and put two stackable bins on either side of the pipes. That one-hour job turns a jumble into two pull-out drawers you can actually use, and everything else — a tension rod, a two-tier shelf, a pull-out organizer — builds on it.
